Peer & architecture review

Eleven-Feature Upgrade Review

Grading the proposed cross-domain mouse-dynamics bot detector against its own Tier 1–4 measurements, the BeCAPTCHA-Mouse paper (Acien et al., 2022), and what commercial CAPTCHA actually does.

DELBOT → Balabit Weka 3.8.6 · RandomForest ×100 reviewer: adversarial 2026-08-29
Bottom line

The four new features are cheap and fast, but they were already tested this cycle and they do not solve the zero-shot gap — added to a DELBOT-only model they push cross-domain bot AUC from 0.53 down to 0.35–0.39 and roughly double the human false-positive rate. The lever that works is training-set augmentation; a feature-matched evasive bot then defeats that; and adversarial training recovers it. A follow-up experiment (§3) shows the remaining 12.4% false-positive rate was a reporting artefact of the default threshold — tuned, the same model catches 58% of evasive bots at 0.97% human FPR. That makes this a credible layered signal and a genuine research contribution about limits. It is still not a standalone CAPTCHA replacement, and it cannot be benchmarked against reCAPTCHA-class systems, which publish no operating points and share no test set.

00

Three premises to correct before scoring

The brief is built on numbers that this project has already superseded. None of the analysis that follows works unless these are fixed first.

The brief says

Baseline: 7-feature RF gives 1.07% FPR on Balabit humans, bot TPR collapses to 1.33%, ROC-AUC ~0.68.

What was measured

Those are the pre-bug-fix artefact numbers. Inter-sample dt was clamped to 1 µs; Balabit logs ~16.5% of samples at tied timestamps, which inflated every velocity/accel/jerk feature up to ~10⁶×. After the fix (collapseToDistinctTimestamps + 1 ms floor) the real baseline is FPR 9.85% (2381 / 24182 chunks), pooled bot AUC 0.5326, TPR@0.5 0.39%, TPR@≤1% FPR 0.00%. The 1.07% reproduces exactly when the fix is reverted; the 0.68 AUC was never reproduced — the closest partial bug-repro lands at 0.62.

The brief says

"The proposed solution: 4 domain-bridging kinematic features" — pause ratio, direction-change frequency, mean angular velocity, accel/decel time ratio.

What was measured

All four are already implemented in Tier2Features.java and measured: pause ratio → pause_ratio; direction-change frequency → dir_reversal_rate; angular velocity → mean_turning_angle / std_turning_angle; accel/decel ratio → time_to_peak_vel_ratio + accel_fraction. This is not a hypothesis to evaluate — it is a completed experiment (Tier 2), and the result is negative (§1.2).

The brief says

Sub-millisecond latency (<0.5 ms per session chunk); zero GPU; in-memory.

What was measured

This one holds. Warm-JVM benchmark, 7-feature model, 20,000 Balabit chunks (~66 points each): feature extraction 10.7 µs + RF inference 13.8 µs = 24.5 µs per chunk (~0.024 ms). One-time model build 552 ms. Caveat: this excludes HTTP/JSON deserialization of the raw event stream, which in practice dominates.

01

Academic: novelty and defensibility vs. BeCAPTCHA-Mouse

1.1  Feature-engineering efficiency

The efficiency argument is real and worth making. BeCAPTCHA-Mouse's neuromotor pipeline fits a Sigma-Lognormal decomposition per trajectory — iterative curve-fitting, and the feature count varies with the number of lognormal strokes recovered. The 11-feature schema here is closed-form timestamp and coordinate arithmetic: one linear pass, ~11 µs measured. For a real-time backend that is a genuine, quantifiable advantage.

But efficiency only counts if accuracy is competitive, and here the comparison breaks down on task, not speed. BeCAPTCHA reports within-domain bot detection — train and test on the same population, against their own synthetic bots. Their headline is accuracy ~93% on one trajectory, ~99% combined, RF 98.7% (Table 5), and their augmentation result is the one this project leans on: train-on-real-only 66.3% → train-on-real+synthetic 96.6% (Table 4). None of that is a cross-dataset number.

Partial

The throughput claim is defensible and easy to quantify. "Matches their performance with cheaper features" is not supported — the 11-feature model does not reach BeCAPTCHA-class accuracy in the setting this project actually tests (cross-domain), and BeCAPTCHA's own within-domain numbers were produced with a much richer representation.

1.2  Zero-shot cross-dataset generalization — the headline result

This is the question the brief wants answered "yes," and the measured answer is no. Tier 2 trained the RandomForest on DELBOT only and evaluated it on Balabit humans + matched synthetic bot twins, across three feature sets:

Adding the domain-bridging features to a DELBOT-only model made cross-domain transfer worse on every axis: pooled AUC dropped below 0.5 (bots now score as more human than the humans), and the human false-positive rate rose from 9.85% to 24–26%. The mechanism is clean: in the enriched space the DELBOT boundary reads "smooth minimum-jerk motion = human, natural hand tremor = bot," and Balabit's real administrative work is full of tremor and direction changes. Scale-normalising the features (SCALEFREE) does not rescue it.

What does work is orthogonal to features. Tier 2's augmentation arm — add one synthetic bot twin per held-out-user training chunk to the DELBOT set — takes pooled bot AUC from 0.53 to 0.998, human FPR from ~10% to ~1%, and it generalises to held-out users and held-out bot types (unseen "*_VP*" variants: AUC 0.92–0.9998). That is the finding to build the paper around — not the feature list.

Not supported

The 11-feature schema does not close the DELBOT→Balabit zero-shot gap. On the measurements it widens it. Claiming otherwise in a journal submission would be contradicted by the project's own logs.

1.3  Theoretical defensibility and hidden flaws

The expansion is defensible as engineering — the features are well-motivated and cheap — but three concrete problems surface under zero-shot transfer:

  • vel_p90_p50_ratio was artefact-dominated — now fixed, and the fix changed nothing. Its mean over real Balabit humans was ~2.3 million. The cause was not velocity spikes (the initial diagnosis): 1.51% of chunks log more than half their steps at the same coordinate, so their median speed is exactly 0 and p90/(0+1e-9) explodes to ~10⁹. The column had degenerated into a duplicate of pause_ratio. Recomputing the ratio over moving steps only gives a sane mean of 13.3 for humans vs 20.3 for the evasive bot — genuinely discriminative. Re-running every tier after the fix moved results by <0.02 AUC and changed no conclusion: the forest was already ignoring the column. A useful robustness check to report.
  • "Task-invariant" is an assumption, not a property. Pause ratio, direction-change frequency and turning statistics are only invariant if the two domains sample motion at comparable rates and the "pause" threshold means the same thing in a 3-second circle task and a 40-minute work session. Tier 3 shows they are not: re-segmenting Balabit to the action level (median 1.6 s / 11 points, much closer to DELBOT's scale) made zero-shot transfer worse still — human FPR 33–68% — because short Balabit actions sit even further outside DELBOT's 100–200-point distribution.
  • Accel/decel time ratio needs a defined peak. With a noisy velocity series and no smoothing, argmax(v) is unstable; on a multi-sub-movement Balabit chunk it is close to meaningless. BeCAPTCHA gets the antagonist/agonist signal because it fits a smooth model first — replacing curve-fitting with argmax on raw samples discards exactly the property being cited.

The honest framing for a paper: these features were tried; here is why hand-crafted kinematics do not transfer; here is what does (augmentation) and how far (§2.2).

02

Commercial: viability vs. reCAPTCHA / Turnstile / hCaptcha

2.1  Market differentiation

The positioning has one strong pillar and two that need qualifying.

Positioning claims, graded
ClaimAssessment
<0.5 ms, zero GPU, in-memory holds  Measured at 24.5 µs core compute. A real operational advantage over deep-model detectors. Publish the benchmark with the request-parsing overhead included so it is not overstated.
"100% privacy-preserving, GDPR-exempt by design, no consent required" overclaim  Mouse-dynamics vectors are personal data under GDPR — the entire Balabit/Antal line of work exists because these signals single out and re-identify individuals, and a biometric used for that purpose can fall under Art. 9 special category. Not storing cookies or a persistent ID reduces obligations; it does not create an exemption. "Consent-free" needs a named lawful basis (legitimate interest) and a DPIA, not a design claim.
"Botnet / proxy immunity — evaluates physical movement, not IP/headers" double-edged  True that it is not fooled by IP rotation. But IP/ASN reputation, TLS and header entropy, and account history are the signals that stop the majority of real bot traffic cheaply. A detector that ignores them is not immune to botnets — it is blind to the layer where botnets are cheapest to catch, and it cannot use cross-session correlation (10k residential IPs all emitting the same trajectory distribution) because it scores each session in isolation.

The defensible market story is "a fast, self-hosted, network-independent behavioural signal to add to a risk engine" — not a replacement for one.

2.2  Production vulnerabilities — the evasion result

Tier 4 answers this question with data rather than speculation. It builds a feature-matched adversarial twin: a bot whose velocity distribution and path efficiency match its source human by construction (verified: mean velocity 8.81 vs 8.88; path efficiency 0.182 vs 0.182). Then it re-runs the augmented model against it.

Tier 4 · held-out users · augmented model vs. naive bots and the evasive twin
Feature setTrainingHuman FPRAUC vs naiveAUC vs evasiveEvasive TPR @≤1% FPR
BASELINE 7+ naive twins1.00%0.99960.5130.7%
AUGMENTED 18+ naive twins0.33%0.99990.5220.5%
AUGMENTED 18+ naive and evasive twins12.39%0.99950.96458.3%

Read the middle row: the model that scores 0.9999 against naive bots is a coin flip (0.52) against a bot that simply reuses the target's own speed and curvature statistics. It learned the naive bots' crude tells and the evasive bot does not have them.

Adding evasive twins to training recovers it — 0.964 AUC — but only with the full 18-feature set. The 12.39% human FPR in that row looks disqualifying, and it is the number the original brief would have to answer for; §3 shows it is an artefact of where the default threshold sits, not of the model. At 7 features the same move flags 31% of humans, so the rich feature set is doing real work here.

The standing caveat is unchanged: the adversarial twin matches only ~10 of 18 features. One that also matched acceleration ordering and curvature would likely pull 0.964 back toward chance, and a reinforcement-learning bot optimising directly against the deployed score is strictly stronger than this hand-built twin and is not tested at all.

Material risk — but bounded

Against a motivated adversary this is an arms race: the naive-bot defence is beaten outright by a bot that copies human feature statistics. Adversarial training does answer that specific attack at a usable operating point (§3), so the position is defensible rather than lost — but only against the attack you already modelled, which is the general condition of this whole field.

2.3  Verdict

Conditional — a strong layered signal, not a standalone gate

Robust: partially — beaten outright by feature-matched evasion unless that attack is in the training set; once it is, 58% of evasive bots are caught at 0.97% human FPR (§3).  Fast: yes — comfortably.  Secure enough to be the CAPTCHA: no — it is blind to the network and reputation layer where most real bot traffic is cheapest to stop.  Secure enough to be one weighted input to a risk engine, behind rate limiting and network reputation: yes, at a tuned threshold with drift monitoring. That is a defensible product claim; "replaces reCAPTCHA" is not.

"Better than existing CAPTCHA models used across websites" is not a measurable claim. reCAPTCHA v3, Turnstile and hCaptcha are multi-signal risk scorers — IP/ASN reputation, fingerprint entropy, account and cookie history, with mouse dynamics as one input among dozens — trained on web-scale proprietary data and updated continuously against live attackers. They publish no FPR/TPR operating points and there is no shared benchmark. If that comparison is a supervisor or committee requirement, it needs renegotiating now, before the manuscript is framed around a claim that cannot be evidenced.

03

Follow-up: the 12.4% false-positive rate was a threshold artefact

The 12.39% human FPR above is the single number that blocks any operational claim, so it was worth attacking directly. It is measured at the model's default decision — but that model's ROC-AUC against the evasive bot is 0.964, which says the scores separate well. A double-digit FPR on top of a 0.964 AUC is a badly chosen operating point, not a broken model.

Four attacks on it, identical held-out user split, same 18 features:

Tier 5 · evasive-bot recall when the threshold is set to hold human FPR at a fixed budget
VariantOwn-default FPREvasive AUC@≤0.5%@≤1%@≤3%@≤5%
PLAIN best12.39%0.964350.1%58.3%73.6%80.3%
CLASS_BALANCED8.94%0.964151.6%57.9%73.5%80.0%
COST_REWEIGHT3.46%0.959246.6%54.4%71.6%78.5%
COST_EXPECTED control0.60%0.964350.1%58.3%73.6%80.3%
Blocker resolved

The unmodified model catches 58.3% of evasive bots and 99.5% of naive bots at 0.97% human false-positive rate — simply by choosing the threshold rather than accepting the default. The target set going in ("~60% evasive recall at under 3% FPR") is met at under 1%.

Two secondary results are worth keeping. First, a clean negative: neither class rebalancing nor cost-sensitive training beats plain threshold tuning at matched FPR. CLASS_BALANCED is statistically indistinguishable (0.9641 vs 0.9643) and COST_REWEIGHT is measurably worse (0.9592) — reweighting the training data cost the forest some ranking quality to buy a lower default threshold, which was free anyway. COST_EXPECTED is included as a control and lands exactly on PLAIN, confirming that applying a cost matrix at inference is threshold tuning under another name.

Second, a reject option is close to free. Deferring sessions whose score falls within ±5% of the range around the threshold: coverage stays at 96.4%, human FPR drops 0.97% → 0.41%, and evasive recall is unchanged at 58.3%. Widening to ±10% gives 91.2% coverage at 0.11% FPR for 53.7% recall. In a layered system where deferred traffic goes to a second signal, that is an excellent trade.

Scope of this result

This fixes the operating point, not the adversarial position. All recall figures are against the modelled evasive bot; a stronger adversary is untested, and the underlying arms-race caveat from §2.2 stands unchanged.

04

What to do next — ranked

  1. Reframe the paper around the augmentation + evasion + operating-point arc.

    The contribution is a rigorous cross-domain protocol and an honest limit: augmentation fixes naive-bot transfer (AUC 0.53→0.998); a feature-matched bot defeats it (0.52); adversarial training recovers it (0.964) and a tuned threshold makes that usable (58% evasive recall at 0.97% FPR). That is a complete, publishable arc. The 11 features become a negative control, not the thesis.

  2. Fix the operating point. done

    Resolved in §3 — 58.3% evasive recall at 0.97% human FPR, with a near-free reject option on top. Cost-sensitive training and class rebalancing were tested and beat nothing.

  3. Fix the velocity artefact. done

    Root cause was a zero-valued median, not velocity spikes (§1.3). Fixed in the Tier-2 feature code only, so the Tier-1 baseline is untouched. All tiers re-run; no conclusion changed.

  4. Fix the remaining raw-velocity artefact — deliberately.

    p99 raw max velocity is 1277 px/ms, which is unphysical: the 1 ms dt floor is too coarse for large position jumps. This one feeds mean_velocity/std_velocity, so fixing it will move the Tier-1 baseline. Do it as an announced re-baseline with before/after numbers, never silently.

  5. Add the classifier sweep only for the benchmark table.

    SVM / kNN / MLP on the same features will not move AUC (the input representation is the problem, not the boundary). Run them for completeness if a reviewer expects it — one afternoon — and say plainly that they do not help.

  6. Rewrite the commercial claims.

    Drop "GDPR-exempt" and "botnet immunity." Position as a self-hosted, low-latency, network-independent behavioural signal for a layered risk engine. Publish the latency benchmark with parsing overhead included.

  7. Then, and only then, consider a sequence model.

    The one genuinely different hypothesis: LSTM/GRU/CNN on raw trajectories rather than aggregates. Within-Balabit authentication, an RF gets AUC ~72% and a deep model ~98% — direct evidence the aggregates discard signal. But cross-domain transfer of deep models is untested in the cited literature, and it needs a non-Weka stack.

Method notes

  • Determinism. Every eval: RandomForest seed=1, numExecutionSlots=1, all File.listFiles() sorted. Confirmed bit-identical across repeat runs.
  • Latency bench. Warm JVM, 20,000 chunks, 7-feature model, single thread. Core compute only — no request deserialization. Java 17 / Weka 3.8.6 (the brief says Java 26; the built artefact is 17).
  • Held-out split. Balabit's 10 users alternated over the sorted list into 5 train / 5 test; no user in both. "Augmentation" numbers are semi-supervised domain adaptation, not zero-shot — the FPR drop partly reflects in-domain human examples in training.
  • Synthetic bots. All from the project's own two synthesizers (Bézier / minimum-jerk / burst-timing, and the feature-matched twin). No captured real bot traffic, no GAN trajectories.
  • A Weka trap worth recording. With minimizeExpectedCost=true, CostSensitiveClassifier's distributionForInstance returns neither probabilities nor expected costs — it argmins the costs and returns a hard one-hot vector. Scored naively that gives two distinct values, so ROC-AUC is meaningless and every fixed-FPR sweep collapses to 0%; the first Tier-5 run reported exactly that. The meaningful score is the underlying forest's P(bot), read via getClassifier().
  • Citation status. Verified against source this cycle: arXiv 2504.21415 (within-dataset Balabit authentication, RF AUC 72% / deep 98%, no cross-dataset test — a prior note mis-cited it as "cross-dataset RF AUC 0.72"), Antal 1810.04668 (Balabit impostor detection, set-of-actions AUC 0.92, drag-drop best action), BeCAPTCHA 2005.00890 (Table 4: 66.3% → 96.6%). Iliou (2021 ×2) and Wei (2019) remain unverified.
