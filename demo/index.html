The way you move is unmistakably alive.

Meander tells people from machines by the shape of their motion — the curve of a cursor, the hesitation before a click, the small correction after overshooting a target.

Living motion has a shape.

A hand reaching for a target accelerates, eases off, drifts a little wide, and corrects. It pauses to think. Automated motion doesn't — it travels in straight lines, at a held speed, or in fragments stitched together out of order.

Meander never asks a visitor to solve anything. It just watches the few seconds of movement that already happen on the way to a button.

Real cursor paths wander and recover. A script's path is efficient in a way a hand never is — or, when it's built to look human, smooth in a way that gives it away just as clearly.

What it reads in those seconds.

Meander splits a session into short bursts of movement and measures about eighteen things about each burst — its geometry and its rhythm — then lets a small forest of decision trees vote.

i.

How it wanders

Total curvature, direction reversals, how far the path strays from the straight line between its ends.

ii.

Where the speed lives

When in the movement the cursor is fastest, how sharply it accelerates, how heavy the tail of the speed distribution is.

iii.

The rhythm of pausing

What fraction of the burst is spent nearly still, and how those stalls are spaced through it.

iv.

The order of things

Whether consecutive steps flow into one another the way real acceleration does, or arrive shuffled.

No single number decides. A hundred small trees each see a slice of the shape, and the majority speaks.
